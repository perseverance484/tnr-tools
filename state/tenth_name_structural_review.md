# The Tenth Name - why it does not hold together, and what to do

Read against the live record. This is a structural critique, not a polish pass. Four things
break, and three of them are the same break.

---

## 1. The artifact contradicts its own distribution

The page is described as the most sensitive thing the enemy owns: nine of our bounty
contracts copied out, plus a serving shinobi predicted to break on a date. Then x17-x18 has
a man selling copies of it for **about what a good meal costs**, who has never read it and
cannot read most of it.

Both cannot be true. A targeting list for recruitment is not street stock. If it circulates
that cheaply and that widely, it is not a secret, and if it is not a secret, the officer's
whole framing collapses.

This is the root break. Two and three below are consequences of it.

## 2. Nobody's motive is stated

- **The enemy.** Why compile it? Why write a date? Nobody has spoken to the tenth person,
  so the list is a plan nobody is executing. A plan with no actor is not a threat, it is a
  curiosity.
- **The officer.** She sends an A rank to retrieve a page whose every fact, the quest later
  reveals, is published by the village itself on walls she signed. She learns nothing from
  the page. The one genuinely new item is a service number she can look up in a minute, and
  she pointedly does not.
- **The seller.** Baffled, honest, sells anything. Good scene, but he has no reason to exist
  in an intelligence operation, only in a market.

## 3. The reveal is the wrong twist, and it is the other quest's twist

"Nobody infiltrated anything, we publish this ourselves" is the same shape as Copies, Not
Thefts - our own material walking out through a door we left open. Two missions, one idea.
Worse, it makes the mission retroactively pointless: if it is all public, the page was never
worth an A rank.

## 4. The endings are epilogues, not outcomes

x26, x27 and x28 are three ways for the officer to lose an argument upstairs, months later,
off screen. x25 tells the player outright their opinion may be ignored. So: five dead-end
or cosmetic route choices, then a final choice explicitly framed as non-binding. The player
never changes anything. That is a defensible ending for one quest; it is not defensible when
it is the only choice in the quest that was ever real.

---

## The fix: it is a market, not an infiltration

One change repairs 1, 2 and 3 at once. **The enemy is not recruiting. They are selling.**

A broker compiles what this village publishes about its own people - who was passed over,
who owes money, who was disciplined - and sells it as a catalogue to anyone who wants
leverage on a serving shinobi. The nine known entries are past sales. **The tenth line is a
listing**: no name because the name is what you pay for, and the date is an estimate of when
that person will be desperate enough to be worth buying.

Everything that was contradictory becomes ordinary:

- Cheap copies circulate widely because a catalogue is *meant* to circulate. The seller who
  cannot read it is not a security failure, he is distribution.
- The enemy's motive is money. The date is a delivery estimate, which is exactly how it
  reads on the page already.
- The officer's motive is concrete: she does not want the page, she wants **the route back
  to the buyer**. Page and route, not the person - the existing line - now means something.
- The village's published lists are still the raw material, so the uncomfortable point
  survives. But the twist is no longer "we did this to ourselves", it is "someone built a
  business on what we publish", which does not collide with Copies, Not Thefts.

### Node-level consequences

**x2-x4 (briefing).** She states the hypothesis: nine names went somewhere and something
happened to each of them afterwards. She wants the buyer. Brief unchanged in wording, now
motivated.

**x9 (the coat).** Keep exactly. Working stock, not a delivery, is even truer for a catalogue
a runner refers to all night.

**x18 (the seller).** Keep the scene, change one beat. He is not baffled that you asked, he
is bored: he has sold six this month. His line stands as written - "They are not secret.
They are just not for you" - and now means *you are not a customer*, which is sharper.

**x20-x21b (the reveal).** The three words stay and the three public sources stay. Add one
beat: the catalogue has **prices** in the margin, and the tenth line's price is the highest
on the page. That is the moment the mission turns, and it is a fact the player finds rather
than a lecture the officer delivers.

**x22-x24 (the officer).** Her problem changes from "we published this" to "somebody has been
paid for nine of ours already, and we never noticed because none of it was stolen."

### The endings, rebuilt as actions

Replace three policy epilogues with three things the player's night actually did:

1. **Buy the tenth listing off the market.** It costs a meal. The listing is gone by morning
   and the broker writes another, because you have just demonstrated that this village pays.
2. **Poison the catalogue.** Feed the chain false entries at the handover you already mapped.
   The next three sales are worthless, the broker's buyers stop trusting the product, and
   somewhere a real name gets missed in the noise - including, possibly, the tenth.
3. **Take the route upstairs.** The buyer is found. It is somebody we already do business
   with, and the file closes above her head within a week.

Each is a different answer to *what do you do about a market* - remove the item, wreck the
product, or follow the money - and each costs something. The tenth person's date stays
unresolved in all three, which keeps the closing line.
